you can find twenty pages explaining what business process automation consulting is. i just read them. they agree with each other, they are all correct, and not one of them shows you the thing you are actually buying.

so here is the deliverable, in the specific. what arrives, what is in it, and how to tell whether the person selling it has ever done the work.

## the deliverable is a document, and you should ask to see its shape before you sign

a real engagement produces a written map of how work moves through your business today, followed by a phased plan for changing it. not a slide deck. a document you could hand to a developer who has never met you, or to an internal team, and they could start.

the map has a row per step. who touches it, what they do, how long it takes, and what happens when it goes wrong. that last column is the one that gets skipped, and it is the one that matters, because the exceptions are where the time actually goes.

then every step gets marked as one of three things: safe to automate, needs a human checkpoint, or should stay manual. three categories, not two. a proposal that has no "should stay manual" column is not an assessment, it is a sales document, and you can stop reading it there.

## the phases have prices, and they are ordered so the first one pays for the next

a plan that quotes one number for everything is asking you to buy the whole theory at once. a good one breaks into phases, prices each, and sequences them so the earliest work returns something before the next phase begins.

that ordering is a real constraint and it changes what gets recommended. the highest-value first phase is very often unglamorous: fixing the intake form, consolidating two systems that both hold customer records, removing a manual re-keying step nobody had noticed. it is rarely the interesting AI piece. if the first phase in your plan is the most technically exciting one, ask why.

## how to tell a real engagement from a deck

**they watch the work happen.** not a workshop where you describe your process. an actual observation of the thing moving, because the described process and the real one are never the same document. the gap between them is most of the finding.

**they tell you what not to automate.** anyone who marks every step green has not looked. judgment calls, anything where being wrong is expensive and being slow is not, and anything a person does that is actually relationship work rather than processing, all of that should come back marked leave it alone.

**the output survives them leaving.** ask directly: if we take this document to another firm, can they execute it? if the answer is soft, the deliverable is a dependency rather than a plan.

**they will name what they could not find out.** a two week look at a business does not produce certainty about everything. an honest map has gaps in it and says so. one with no gaps is either a very small business or a document written to close.

## what i charge, and how long it takes

i price a fixed scope assessment at $2,500, credited in full against the build if you proceed within ninety days. it runs two weeks, and it takes about three to four hours of your team's time in total, spread across them.

i am telling you my number rather than describing a market rate, because i have not measured the market and neither has anyone quoting you one.

be suspicious of free. an assessment given away is priced into the build you have not agreed to yet, and it creates an incentive to find work rather than to find the truth. paying for the plan is also the cheapest qualification available to you in both directions: a firm that will not sell you the plan on its own only makes money if you buy the build.

## the one question that sorts them

ask what happens when the automation is unsure.

the wrong answer is a confident one about accuracy. the right answer is a description of a fallback: the work stops, it routes to a named person, and the reason is recorded. a system that guesses when it cannot extract cleanly is not degrading gracefully, it is hiding a failure behind a plausible answer, and you will not find out for months.

anyone who has actually shipped this will answer that question immediately, because it is the part they had to build.
