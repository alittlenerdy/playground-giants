it is a tuesday morning. your "ai solution" vendor just closed their doors. you need to know who has access to your client data, how your intake queue will run, and where your systems actually live. you cannot find anyone to ask.

this is not a hypothetical for shock value. it is the predictable end state of how most firms buy AI right now. someone sells you a workflow, builds it fast, and hosts it somewhere you've never seen. it works, until it doesn't, and then you learn what you actually bought: access to a system, not ownership of one.

## the "solution" that becomes a problem

the pitch is always some version of the same thing: don't worry about the infrastructure, we handle all of that. and at first, it feels like a gift. no cloud accounts to set up, no engineers to hire, no DevOps headache. the vendor spins up their own AWS or Azure environment, builds your intake automation or document routing inside it, and hands you a login.

the problem shows up later, and it shows up all at once. the system lives in the vendor's account, under the vendor's billing, behind the vendor's credentials. you have a user seat, not a deed. if the vendor raises prices, you pay. if the vendor pivots to a different market, you're deprioritized. if the vendor shuts down, disappears, or gets acquired by someone who guts the product, your operational workflow, the one your staff now depends on daily, goes down with them. you didn't buy infrastructure. you rented a black box with your firm's name taped to the front.

## why "just export the data" is not enough

when people worry about vendor dependency, they usually frame it as a data problem. "make sure you can export your data" is common advice, and it's not wrong, it's just incomplete in a way that matters a lot for regulated work.

data is the record of what happened. infrastructure is the thing that makes things happen. a CSV of your past intake decisions doesn't run your intake queue tomorrow morning. the routing logic, the extraction prompts, the deterministic rules that decided which matter got flagged for partner review, the audit trail schema, the retry behavior when a model call fails: none of that travels in an export. you can own every byte of your historical data and still have zero ability to operate your practice the day the vendor's servers go dark.

this distinction matters more in regulated work than almost anywhere else, because the thing you need to reconstruct isn't just "what we knew," it's "how the decision got made." if the mechanism that made the decision lived entirely inside someone else's account, you can't reconstruct it. you can only ask them nicely to send you a copy, assuming they still exist and still want to help.

## auditing the "black box"

regulated industries live and die on the ability to answer a simple question: how did this decision happen. a bar association, a malpractice carrier, or a client's own attorney can ask a firm to show its work. "the vendor's system decided" is not an answer anyone wants to give under oath.

but if the system runs in a vendor-controlled account, you often can't answer that question yourself. you don't have log access. you don't have visibility into which model version processed a given intake, what prompt it ran against, or whether a human reviewed the output before it became a decision. you're auditing a black box from the outside, which is another way of saying you're not auditing it at all. you're trusting.

trust is fine for a lot of vendor relationships. it is not fine for the system that decides whether a potential client's claim gets flagged for statute-of-limitations review, or whether a document gets routed to the person licensed to make the call on it.

## how to build on your terms

the alternative is not complicated, it's just less convenient for the vendor. infrastructure gets built inside the client's own cloud accounts. your AWS. your Azure. your GCP. the vendor writes the code and configures the pipeline, but the account, the billing, the access controls, and the logs belong to you from day one.

this flips the entire relationship. you can see every step a piece of work took, because the logs are in your account, not requested from someone else's. you can restrict access the way you'd restrict access to anything else in your firm, because it's subject to your existing security policy, not a vendor's. and critically, the actual decision in any regulated workflow should be made by a deterministic function that a person can read line by line, not by a model's best guess. the model reads a document, extracts what matters, and routes it. it does not make the call. that split only means something if you can actually open the code and see it, which you can, because it's sitting in your own account.

## the handover: a clean break, not a hostage negotiation

here's the test that matters: what happens on the day the consultant leaves.

if the infrastructure sits in the vendor's account, "the day the consultant leaves" is a migration project. someone has to extract the logic, rebuild the pipeline somewhere you control, re-test it, and hope nothing was lost in translation. that takes weeks, sometimes months, and during that window your workflow either breaks or reverts to fully manual, which is exactly the failure mode you built the system to avoid.

if the infrastructure already lives in your account, the same day looks completely different. you revoke the consultant's access. you receive documentation of what was built and how it runs. the system keeps running, because it was never dependent on the consultant's continued existence to function, only on their continued involvement to improve it. leaving is a handover, not a hostage negotiation.

that's the whole test, honestly. ask any vendor selling you an AI system one question: whose cloud account does this run in. if the answer isn't yours, you haven't bought infrastructure. you've bought a dependency with a monthly invoice attached.
